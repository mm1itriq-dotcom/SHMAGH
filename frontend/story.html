The Nabataeans: Masters of Rock
and Water in the Heart of the
Desert

Petra flourished as the capital of the Nabataeans since the 4th century BC, at the crossroads of caravan routes between the Arabian Peninsula and the Mediterranean. From the heart of the rose mountains, they built a city combining trade, rituals, and art in a scene unlike any other.

The Nabataeans mastered water engineering in a rain-scarce environment; they carved channels, reservoirs, and dams, turning flash floods into a life resource. Their majestic facades blended Eastern traditions with Hellenistic and Roman influences.

Their legacy remains carved in stone and in the memory of the place.

Umm Qais: Where History Meets Nature

TALE OF THE PLACE

Umm Qais is distinguished by its charming view of the Sea of Galilee and the Golan Heights. Where the remains of the ancient city embrace green plains extending to the horizon. Known in the Greek era as "Gadara", it was one of the ten Decapolis cities and a thriving center for thought, poetry, and trade.

Its paved streets and black basalt columns reveal successive layers of Greek, Roman, and Byzantine civilizations. The Western Theater, carved into volcanic stone, stands as a witness to a rich cultural life that gathered the city's people and its visitors many centuries ago.

In Umm Qais, history is inseparable from nature; between Ottoman houses, baths, and temples, scenes open up to valleys and olive orchards, and at sunset, golden light covers the ancient stones, making the city seem like a living memory telling the story of the place and the human.

The Roman Theater: Echo of History

MEMORY OF THE CITY

The Roman Theater is considered one of the most important historical landmarks in the heart of the capital Amman, and a stone witness to the city's prosperity in the Roman era. Built in the second century AD, when Amman was known as Philadelphia, to welcome thousands of spectators in a majestic architectural space embraced by Mount Al-Joufeh.

Its seating was carved into the rock with a precise curve that gives the place exceptional acoustic properties; sounds reached the upper rows with amazing clarity. At its facade stand the remains of columns and corridors as a visual rhythm telling the brilliance of the builders, while the colors of the stone change with daylight from honey to bronze.

Today, the theater remains a living space for culture and memory, where concerts and festivals sit side by side with the footsteps of visitors, in a rare meeting between contemporary Amman and its ancient past; a place not only to be seen, but to listen to the echo of a history spanning centuries.

A Stage of Roman Amman

Second Century AD

During the Roman era, the theater was a major public space in Philadelphia, used for performances, public gatherings, and civic spectacles. Its large stone seating allowed thousands of people to gather and experience events together.

A Witness Through the Centuries

Through the Centuries

Through the centuries, the theater remained a remarkable witness to the changing history of Amman. Its ancient stone seats and architecture survived generations of change, preserving a connection between the city's Roman past and its later history.

A Living Landmark

Modern-Day Amman

Today, the Roman Theater remains one of Amman's most important historical landmarks. Under Jordan's national heritage and cultural care, the site continues to be preserved and used.
